Seven questions to ask before accepting a load

Published August 27, 2026 · 7 min read

Most load-booking surprises are preventable. Detention that runs long, equipment that doesn't match the freight, or a delivery appointment that quietly turns into a two-day commitment — almost all of it traces back to a detail that wasn't confirmed before the load was accepted, not to bad luck.

These seven questions take a few minutes to run through and catch the vast majority of avoidable problems.

1. What are the exact pickup and delivery appointment times?

"FCFS" (first come, first served) and a hard appointment window are very different commitments. Confirm which one you're actually dealing with, and whether the delivery time is a firm appointment or an estimate — late arrival penalties depend on knowing this in advance.

2. How many free hours before detention pay starts, and what's the rate?

Get this in writing on the rate confirmation, not verbally. Ask specifically about the number of free hours, the hourly detention rate after that, and whether there's a cap on total detention pay.

3. Is this a live load/unload, or drop and hook?

This affects both your time commitment and your risk. A live unload ties up your truck and driver time at the dock; drop and hook lets you keep moving. Neither is automatically better, but you need to know which one you're planning around.

4. Does the equipment match what's required?

Confirm trailer type, any required certifications (food-grade, hazmat endorsement, etc.), tarping requirements for flatbed, and temperature range for reefer loads. A mismatch discovered at the shipper costs hours you won't get paid for.

Also worth confirming: any weight restrictions, oversized/overweight permit requirements, and whether the freight requires specific securement equipment you may not already have on hand.

5. What are the lumper fee and accessorial terms?

Ask who pays lumper fees, whether they're reimbursed and how quickly, and whether any other accessorial charges (pallet exchange, inside delivery, liftgate) apply. These add up and are easy to miss until they show up as a deduction later.

6. When and how will you actually get paid?

Standard payment terms, quick-pay availability and its fee, and who to contact if payment is delayed are all worth confirming before the load is delivered — not after, when you have far less leverage.

7. Is there a realistic path to a next load from the delivery location?

This isn't about the current load's rate — it's about what happens after. A quick check of typical outbound freight from the delivery area tells you whether you're likely to find a reasonable next load nearby, or whether you should factor a longer, unpaid repositioning move into this load's real economics.

Putting it into practice

None of these questions require a long phone call. Most brokers and dispatchers can answer all seven in a couple of minutes, and a broker who's evasive about detention terms or equipment requirements is telling you something useful about how the rest of the load is likely to go.

The goal isn't to slow down booking — it's to make sure the load you accept is the load you thought you were accepting.

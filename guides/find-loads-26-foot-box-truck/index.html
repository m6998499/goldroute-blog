Box truck load strategy

How to Find Loads for a 26-Foot Box Truck

Published October 1, 2026 · 7 min read

To find loads for a 26-foot box truck, start with freight searches that match box truck or straight truck equipment, limit pickups to a realistic radius, and confirm the freight fits your truck before booking. Then compare the load's revenue against all the miles and time the trip will require.

The most useful search is specific: where your truck is available, when it can pick up, what it can safely carry, and where you want to deliver. A longer list of loads is helpful only when those loads fit your operation.

Where to find 26-foot box truck loads

Use three channels together: load boards, broker carrier opportunities, and direct shipper relationships. Each serves a different purpose.

  • Load boards: Search using box truck, straight truck, or relevant equipment categories when the board offers them. Read the equipment requirements on every listing. A dry van listing does not automatically accept a box truck.
  • Broker carrier portals: Look for published carrier registration pages and equipment requirements. Complete onboarding accurately, then identify freight that matches your equipment, lanes, and availability.
  • Direct local customers: Research manufacturers, distributors, warehouses, and retailers with delivery needs your truck could serve. A short email or vendor inquiry can describe your service area, dimensions, loading equipment, and available days.

For recurring work, ask about the actual route: number of stops, delivery windows, handling duties, frequency, and payment terms. A repeat route still needs to pass the same equipment and cost checks as a one-time shipment.

Build an equipment profile before you search

“26-foot box truck” describes a size category, not a universal payload or loading setup. Penske's 26-foot truck specifications, for example, explain that capacities and dimensions are approximate and can vary by manufacturer. Use the specifications and actual measurements for your own truck.

Keep these details in a reusable note. When a listing is incomplete, get the missing requirements confirmed before accepting it. Do not assume that a pallet count alone proves the load will fit.

Search a workable pickup radius and destination

Begin with your current city or ZIP code, the date your truck is available, and a pickup radius you can afford. If results are sparse, change one variable at a time: a nearby pickup market, a different date, or a destination within your preferred service area.

A distant pickup can turn an attractive posted rate into a weak trip. Before expanding the radius, estimate the empty miles, fuel, and time needed to get there. Our guide to reducing deadhead miles explains how to evaluate that tradeoff.

Check the delivery market too. Search for equipment-compatible outbound freight near the destination before committing. Treat a possible return load as an opportunity until it is actually confirmed; do not build the trip's budget around an unbooked backhaul.

Compare the load using all miles and time

A posted rate per loaded mile leaves out the trip to pickup and any repositioning after delivery. Include those miles when comparing offers.

All-mile revenue per mile = total load revenue ÷ (pickup deadhead + loaded miles + planned empty repositioning).

For illustration, a $650 load with 240 loaded miles appears to pay about $2.71 per loaded mile. Add 40 miles to pickup and 20 planned empty miles after delivery, and the trip becomes 300 miles. Its all-mile revenue is about $2.17 per mile. These are example numbers, not a market rate or earnings estimate.

Compare the result with your own fuel, maintenance, tires, insurance, truck payments, driver pay, and other operating costs. Also account for tolls, loading labor, extra stops, waiting, and overnight expenses where relevant.

Time can change the decision. Two loads with similar mileage may occupy very different amounts of your day. Confirm the appointments, handling duties, detention terms, and any extra charges in writing. Use our load evaluation guide and rate per mile versus profit guide for a fuller comparison.

Verify who is offering the freight

Before booking with an unfamiliar counterparty, check that the company identity and contact details match official records. FMCSA's broker and carrier fraud guidance recommends confirming broker and carrier phone numbers through SAFER. If the supplied number differs, use the number in the official record to verify the transaction.

Review the rate confirmation for the company name, pickup and delivery details, agreed charges, and payment terms. Resolve identity mismatches or unexplained changes before dispatching. Keep the confirmation and delivery paperwork together so you can follow the payment process accurately.

A repeatable daily search routine

  1. Update your truck's location, availability, usable cargo space, and available payload.
  2. Search compatible equipment within your starting pickup radius.
  3. Shortlist loads whose freight, access, appointments, and handling duties fit.
  4. Calculate all-mile revenue and estimate the trip's costs and total time.
  5. Check the destination and verify the company offering the load.
  6. Confirm the booking terms in writing before moving the truck.
  7. Record actual miles, waiting, costs, and payment timing after delivery.

Over time, those records can show which lanes and customers work for your truck. Keep a simple log of the searches that produce suitable freight and the reasons other loads did not fit. That makes the next search more focused.

Common questions about box truck loads

Can a 26-foot box truck haul any dry van load?

No. Verify the required equipment, freight dimensions and weight, loading access, and service requirements. Space in the box is only one part of the decision. Get explicit confirmation that your truck is accepted for that shipment.

Do I need a liftgate to find box truck freight?

It depends on the shipment. Ground-level deliveries may require one; other loads may use docks or site equipment. Confirm loading and unloading arrangements at both ends, along with any driver handling duties, before booking.

How do I choose between local and longer-distance loads?

Compare the complete trip, including empty miles, time, stops, expenses, and what happens after delivery. Local work can involve multiple stops and handling; a longer run can create return-trip costs. Choose the load that fits your equipment and operating plan.

How to evaluate a freight load before you book it

Published August 27, 2026 · 8 min read

A posted rate tells you almost nothing on its own. $2.85 a mile sounds fine until you factor in a 120-mile deadhead to pick it up, a two-hour detention with no pay past the first hour, and a delivery window that eats your next day's availability. The number that matters isn't the rate on the board — it's what the load actually contributes to your week once every cost and constraint is accounted for.

Here's a repeatable way to check that before you commit, whether you're working a load board, a broker relationship, or a direct shipper.

Start with total miles, not loaded miles

Every load has two mileage numbers: the loaded miles from pickup to delivery, and the miles you'll drive to get empty to that pickup in the first place. If you're sitting in Baltimore and the load originates in Harrisburg, that's deadhead you're paying fuel and time for with zero revenue attached.

Add both together to get your true trip miles, then divide the total rate by that number — not just the loaded miles. This single adjustment is usually where a load's real economics show up. A $900 load that looks like $3.00/mile on 300 loaded miles can quietly become $2.35/mile once 90 deadhead miles are folded in.

Price in the time, not just the miles

Miles and hours don't always move together. A short-haul load with a tight delivery appointment, a live unload, or a multi-stop itinerary can consume most of a day while only paying for 150 miles of driving. Ask yourself: what else could I be doing with the hours this load will actually take, door to door, including any wait?

If a load ties up your truck for 10 hours to deliver 200 miles, compare that to a similar-mileage load you could run in 4–5 hours. Same miles, very different day.

Quick check: divide total revenue by total hours committed (drive time + loading/unloading + expected wait), not just by miles. A rate-per-hour view catches problems a rate-per-mile view misses.

Know the detention and accessorial terms before you accept

Detention pay, layover pay, and any accessorial charges should be confirmed — in writing, ideally on the rate confirmation — before you commit, not discovered after two hours at the dock. Ask specifically:

  • How many free hours before detention pay starts?
  • What's the detention rate per hour?
  • Is there a cap on how much detention will be paid?
  • Are there live-unload or lumper fees, and who's responsible for them?

A shipper or broker who can't answer these clearly, or who's vague about it, is telling you something about how the rest of the load will go.

Check where the load leaves you

The best load isn't always the one with the highest posted rate — it's the one that leaves you in a strong position for the next one. A load that drops you in a freight-dense market with strong outbound volume is worth more to your week than a slightly higher-paying load that strands you somewhere with thin outbound options and a long, unpaid drive to find your next pickup.

Before booking, it's worth a quick look at outbound freight from the delivery area. If you consistently end up dead-heading a long way out of certain delivery markets, that's a pattern worth tracking and factoring into how you value loads going into those areas.

Put it together: a simple pre-booking checklist

  • Total rate ÷ total miles (loaded + deadhead) = your real rate per mile
  • Total rate ÷ total hours committed = your real rate per hour
  • Detention, layover, and accessorial terms confirmed in writing
  • Delivery location's outbound freight strength considered
  • Appointment times and any multi-stop requirements confirmed

None of this needs to take more than a few minutes once it's a habit. The carriers who consistently run profitable weeks aren't necessarily finding better-paying loads — they're more consistently rejecting loads that only look good until the deadhead and the clock get factored in.

A load board that shows you deadhead distance and estimated trip time alongside the rate makes this whole process faster, since you're not calculating it by hand for every load you're considering.

How to reduce deadhead miles and protect your profit

Published September 1, 2026 · 8 min read

Empty miles can quietly turn a good-looking freight rate into a weak trip. When your truck moves without revenue attached, you are still paying for fuel, maintenance, tires, insurance, depreciation, and your time. That is why successful owner-operators look beyond loaded miles and pay close attention to deadhead.

The goal is not to eliminate every empty mile. That is rarely realistic. The goal is to make deadhead a deliberate business decision instead of an expense you discover after accepting the load.

Calculate the load using all miles

Start by adding the distance from your current location to pickup to the loaded miles from pickup to delivery. Divide the gross load rate by that total. This gives you an all-mile rate that reflects the trip more accurately than the posted loaded-mile rate.

For example, a $1,000 load covering 400 loaded miles appears to pay $2.50 per mile. If you must drive 100 miles empty to pick it up, however, the truck actually travels 500 miles. Your all-mile revenue falls to $2.00 per mile before operating costs.

Quick check: Gross load rate ÷ (deadhead miles + loaded miles) = all-mile revenue per mile.

Set a deadhead limit before searching

Instead of evaluating deadhead only after finding an attractive load, establish a maximum pickup radius that fits your operating costs and market. A tighter radius can prevent high posted rates from distracting you from the unpaid miles required to reach the freight.

Your limit does not have to be identical in every market. A longer deadhead may make sense when it moves you toward a strong freight lane, gets you home, or positions the truck for a valuable follow-up load. The important point is that the additional miles should have a business reason.

Evaluate the destination before accepting the load

Deadhead risk exists at both ends of a trip. A load with very little pickup deadhead can still be expensive if it delivers into an area where your next realistic freight opportunity is 100 miles away.

Before accepting a load, look at the destination as the starting point for your next move. Consider whether outbound freight is commonly available, whether the market fits your equipment, and how far you may have to reposition if nothing useful appears.

Think in sequences instead of individual loads

One load rarely determines whether your week is profitable. A sequence of loads does. A slightly lower-paying load that delivers into a stronger freight market can sometimes create a better two-load or three-load sequence than a higher-paying load that leaves the truck stranded.

This is why route planning and load selection should work together. When possible, compare loads based on where they position the truck next, not simply which one displays the largest rate today.

Track your empty-mile percentage

At the end of each week, total your loaded miles and deadhead miles. Then divide deadhead miles by total miles traveled. Tracking this consistently gives you a baseline and helps identify lanes, customers, or markets that repeatedly create unnecessary repositioning.

If the percentage begins climbing, review why. You may be chasing distant pickups, delivering too frequently into weak outbound markets, or accepting loads without considering the next move.

Know when deadhead is worth paying for

Not every empty mile is bad. Strategic deadhead can be worthwhile when it leads to substantially better freight, gets the truck into a preferred lane, supports a home-time requirement, or avoids a market where waiting would cost more than repositioning.

The distinction is intentionality. If you know the cost and expected benefit before moving the truck, deadhead becomes part of the plan rather than a surprise.

A simple deadhead checklist

  • Calculate revenue using loaded miles plus pickup deadhead
  • Check likely repositioning miles after delivery
  • Set a reasonable pickup radius for the current market
  • Compare the destination's outbound freight opportunities
  • Evaluate the next load, not only the current one
  • Track deadhead as a percentage of total weekly miles

Reducing empty miles does not require finding a perfect load every time. It requires consistently seeing the miles that do not appear in the advertised rate. A load board that makes pickup distance and route information easier to compare can help owner-operators make those decisions faster.

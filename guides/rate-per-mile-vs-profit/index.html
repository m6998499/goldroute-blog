Rate per mile is not profit: what carriers should calculate

Published August 27, 2026 · 9 min read

Rate per mile is the number every load board shows first, and it's the number most carriers use to compare loads at a glance. The problem is that it only counts loaded miles and ignores almost everything else that determines whether a load actually made you money — deadhead, fixed costs, time, and what happens after delivery.

None of this means rate per mile is useless. It's just incomplete. Here's how to build a fuller picture without turning load evaluation into a spreadsheet project.

Start with your true operating cost per mile

Before you can judge whether a load is profitable, you need a baseline: what does it cost you to run one mile, all-in? This includes fuel, maintenance and repair reserves, insurance, truck payment (or depreciation if it's paid off), permits, and a reasonable allowance for your own time. Many owner-operators land somewhere in the $1.50–$1.80 per mile range for total operating cost, though it varies significantly by equipment, region, and how the truck is financed.

Once you know your cost per mile, every rate-per-mile number on a load board becomes meaningful. A $2.10/mile load isn't automatically good — it depends entirely on what your mile costs to run.

Fold deadhead into the calculation, always

The rate per mile shown on a load board is almost always calculated against loaded miles only. Your actual cost structure doesn't care whether the mile was loaded or empty — fuel and wear happen either way. Take the total rate, divide it by total miles (loaded plus deadhead to pickup), and compare that number — not the posted one — against your operating cost per mile.

Example: A load pays $1,800 for 650 loaded miles ($2.77/mile posted). If picking it up requires 85 deadhead miles, the real number is $1,800 ÷ 735 total miles = $2.45/mile — still solid, but noticeably different from the posted rate.

Account for the time the load actually consumes

Two loads with identical all-in rates per mile are not equal if one takes 9 hours door-to-door and the other takes 13 because of a live unload, a multi-stop route, or a tight delivery window that forces a layover. Time is a real cost — it's capacity you don't have available for the next load.

A rough rate-per-hour figure (total revenue ÷ total hours committed, including loading and unloading) is a useful second filter alongside rate per mile. It tends to catch loads that look fine on a per-mile basis but quietly eat a disproportionate amount of your available driving day.

Factor in what the load sets up next

A load's profitability doesn't end at the delivery dock. Where it leaves you matters. A load that pays slightly less but delivers into a freight-dense market with strong outbound options can be more profitable over a full week than a higher-paying load that strands you somewhere with weak outbound freight and a long, unpaid drive to find the next one.

This is easy to overlook when comparing two loads side by side, since neither the posted rate nor the per-mile figure captures it. It's worth building the habit of asking, before booking: where does this leave me, and what does outbound freight typically look like from there?

A simple framework to use going forward

  • Know your true operating cost per mile (fuel, maintenance, insurance, truck payment, your time)
  • Always calculate rate against total miles — loaded plus deadhead — not just loaded miles
  • Check rate per hour, not just rate per mile, for loads with unusual time demands
  • Weigh where the load delivers you and the outbound freight strength there
  • Compare the resulting number against your cost baseline, not against the posted rate alone

Rate per mile will always be the fastest way to scan a load board. Used alongside these adjustments, it becomes a genuinely useful profitability signal instead of a number that can quietly mislead.
